Sensation vs perception

Sensation

Detecting

Your sense organs pick up raw energy from the world and turn it into electrical signals. For vision, light lands on the retina and receptors (rods and cones) fire. There is no meaning yet, just data.

Like a camera sensor recording dots of light.

Perception

Interpreting

The brain organises and interprets those signals, using cues, memory and expectation. This is how you end up experiencing objects, distance and meaning.

Like thinking "that is my friend, about five metres away".

Memory hook: sensation is what arrives. Perception is what you make of it.

Visual cues

The retina is a flat surface, so the image on it is 2D. The brain works out depth and distance using depth cues. Monocular cues work with one eye. Binocular cues need two.

Monocular cues: build a scene

Everything starts switched off. Turn cues on one at a time and watch what changes in the picture.

Cues on: 0 of 4
Height in the plane

Objects higher up in the visual field look further away.

Relative size

If two objects are the same real size, the one with the smaller retinal image looks further away.

Occlusion

If one object partly blocks another, the blocked one looks further away.

Linear perspective

Parallel lines, like railway tracks, seem to meet in the distance. The closer together they look, the further away that part seems.

Binocular cues: retinal disparity and convergence

Your eyes are about 6 cm apart, so each gets a slightly different view. Drag the slider to move the object closer or further.

Top-down view: convergence
What each eye sees: retinal disparity
Retinal disparity

The difference between the images in the left and right eye. The brain compares them. A bigger difference means the object is closer.

Convergence

Your eyes turn inwards to focus on near objects. The brain senses the muscle tension. More inward turn means the object is closer.

Try it: hold a finger at arm's length and close each eye in turn. Your finger jumps sideways, but the far wall barely moves. Binocular cues work best for things within a few metres.

Constancy: size constancy

As someone walks away, the image on your retina gets much smaller. Yet you do not think they are shrinking. Size constancy is perceiving an object as the same size even when its retinal image changes. The brain uses depth cues to scale the size back up.

Retinal image size
Perceived size

Why this matters: when depth cues are misleading, the brain scales size wrongly and you get illusions such as the Ponzo and the Ames room (see section 5).

Gibson's direct theory

Gibson (1966, 1979) said the real world already contains enough information for us to perceive it directly, with no need for inference, memory or guesses. Perception is bottom-up: it starts with the sensory information and builds up from there.

Optic array

The pattern of light reaching the eye from the environment. It carries rich information such as texture gradients, which get finer with distance.

Optic flow

The pattern of movement you see as you move. Everything seems to stream outwards from the point you are heading towards. Pilots use this to land aircraft.

Optic flow

Press play and move forwards. Speed up and the flow gets faster. Move backwards and it reverses.

Motion parallax

When you move your head or travel, near objects seem to move fast and far objects seem to move slowly. The brain reads depth straight from this. Drag the slider as if you were walking past.

Evaluating Gibson

Strengths
  • Real-world applications. Pilot training uses optic flow and texture gradients.
  • Explains everyday perception of movement and depth, which happens fast.
  • Studies real scenes, so it has high ecological validity.
Weaknesses
  • Cannot explain visual illusions. Gibson said these are artificial 2D drawings missing real-world cues.
  • Ignores the role of memory, expectation and experience (perceptual set).
  • Does not say how the brain actually picks up the information.

Gregory's constructivist theory

Gregory (1970) said the information reaching the eye is often incomplete or ambiguous, so the brain constructs a model of reality. It uses inferences from visual cues and past experience to form a best guess, called a perceptual hypothesis. Perception is top-down: knowledge shapes what we see.

1 Cues

Incomplete signals arrive from the retina.

2 Past experience

The brain checks them against stored knowledge.

3 Hypothesis

It picks the most likely explanation. Usually it is right. Sometimes it is wrong, and that is an illusion.

Evaluating Gregory

Strengths
  • Explains why visual illusions happen.
  • Explains perceptual set, culture and expectation effects (section 6).
  • Explains how we cope with incomplete or ambiguous information.
Weaknesses
  • Perception is usually fast and accurate, even for new things. A guessing system should make more mistakes.
  • Illusions use artificial stimuli, so they may not show how perception works in real life.
  • Gibson argues the real world already holds enough information.

Perceptual set and what shapes it

Perceptual set is a mental readiness to perceive something in a particular way. It is shaped by expectation, motivation, emotion and culture. It fits Gregory's idea that past experience guides perception.

Expectation

Bruner and Minturn (1955)

Aim: to see if expectation affects how an ambiguous figure is perceived.

Method: participants were shown a figure that could be the letter B or the number 13. Some saw it in a sequence of letters, others in a sequence of numbers.

Results: those shown letters tended to see the figure as B. Those shown numbers tended to see 13.

Conclusion: expectation sets us up to perceive an ambiguous stimulus in a particular way.

Try your own expectation. Read this phrase once, then answer.

Evaluation: a controlled experiment, but the figure is artificial, so it may not reflect real life.

Motivation

Gilchrist and Nesberg (1952)

Aim: to see if need (hunger and thirst) changes how bright need-related pictures look.

Method: participants went without food or water for different lengths of time. They then adjusted the brightness of projected pictures, some of food and drink and some of other things, until they matched what they saw.

Brightness set for food pictures
Brightness set for neutral pictures

This slider shows the shape of the finding, not the original numbers.

Results: the longer participants had gone without, the brighter they rated pictures of food or drink. Neutral pictures were not affected.

Conclusion: motivation changes perception. Things that meet our needs seem more vivid.

Evaluation: deprivation raises ethical concerns. Brightness matching is subjective, and the lab setting was artificial.

Emotion

Kunst-Wilson and Zajonc (1980)

Aim: to see if emotion (liking) can be influenced by shapes we have seen, even if we do not know we saw them.

Method: participants saw irregular shapes flashed for just 1 millisecond. Later they saw pairs, one old and one new. They said which they liked more and which they had seen before.

Correct at saying which was seen (about 48%, chance level)
Preferred the old shape (about 60%)

Conclusion: people liked the shapes they had seen even though they could not recognise them. Emotion can shape perception without awareness (the mere exposure effect).

Evaluation: a lab study with meaningless shapes, so it lacks ecological validity. It was a controlled experiment, so the cause and effect is clear.

Culture

Nisbett and Miyamoto (2005)

Aim: to review whether culture affects how people perceive scenes.

Findings: people from East Asian cultures such as Japan tend to perceive holistically, paying attention to the context and the relationships between things. Westerners, such as Americans, tend to perceive analytically, focusing on the main object and separating it from its background.

Frame-and-line task (the sort of task used in this research, for example by Kitayama and colleagues, 2003). Look at the line in the first frame, then redraw it in a new frame.
Choose a task type, then press the button.

Research reviewed in this area found East Asian participants tended to do better at the relative version, and Americans at the absolute one.

Evaluation: shows culture shapes perception, which supports Gregory. But the groups are broad, it is correlational, and the tasks are artificial.

How this connects

All four studies show that perception is not just a copy of the sensory input. Experience, needs, feelings and culture all change what we perceive. This supports Gregory's top-down theory and is a challenge for Gibson's purely direct view.
